## Source-quality warning, stated before the numbers

Market sizing for AI in education is **unusually unreliable**. This pass's sizing leg returned
2026 global estimates spanning **$9.58B to $23B** — a 2.4× spread — from publishers who do not
agree on whether "AI in education" means tutoring software, all edtech with an AI feature, or
institutional AI spend. Several of the sources are aggregator blogs restating each other.

**Use the figures below as directional only.** Where a client decision depends on the number,
commission the primary report. The *structural* findings in this file (who regulates what,
when obligations bite, which platforms are deployed where) are far more robust than the
dollar figures and should carry more weight in an engagement.

## Measured adoption by region — new in `p872`

This is the section `p871` could not write. Unlike the dollar figures above, these are **survey
measurements with stated sample sizes**, and they should carry more weight in an engagement than
any market-size estimate in this file.

### The single most useful comparator found

UNESCO IESALC reports that **45% of higher-education institutions in Latin America and the
Caribbean have formal AI guidance, against 70% in Europe and North America.** That is a
like-for-like regional governance gap of **25 percentage points** from one instrument — the only
clean cross-region comparison this KB holds.

### Global baseline

| Measure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Students using AI | **88%** | Digital Education Council, *AI in Higher Education Global Survey 2026* — **45,398 responses across 35 countries** |
| Faculty using AI | **77%** | Same; both figures up ~16pp on 2025 |
| Faculty who say their institution meaningfully involves them in shaping AI policy | **31%** | Same |
| Faculty *intending* to keep using AI in teaching | **67%, down from 76% in 2025** | Same — a **9pp fall** |

**That 9-point decline is the most important number in this file.** Faculty use is still rising
while faculty *intent* is falling: adoption is being driven by circumstance, not conviction. A
services engagement that treats faculty as willing adopters will mis-price its change-management
effort.

### North America — the governance vacuum is measured, not asserted

| Measure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| US administrators reporting an institution-wide AI policy | **32%** | Tyton Partners, 2026 |
| US provosts reporting a centralised AI strategy | **~1 in 10** | Inside Higher Ed, 2026 |
| Instructors reporting institutional written AI guidelines | **<50%** | AAC&U / Elon University, 1,000+ instructors, fall 2025 |
| Faculty who wrote **their own** policy on student AI use | **87%** | Same |
| US & Canada faculty who believe AI poses a serious risk to human intellectual development | **55%** | Digital Education Council 2026 — the global faculty-intent decline is **concentrated here** |

**Read those five rows together.** Roughly a third of institutions have a policy, one in ten has
a strategy, and **87% of faculty have written their own** — so North American institutions are
not un-governed, they are governed by a thousand incompatible syllabus-level rules. Consolidating
that into one defensible institutional position is a concrete, sizeable, immediately sellable
engagement, and it is a *policy and change* engagement before it is a software one.

**Caveat, stated:** these four instruments measure different respondents (administrators,
provosts, instructors) and different objects (policy, strategy, guidelines). They must not be
averaged or treated as a trend line. The AAC&U/Elon survey is self-described as non-scientific.

| Measure | Value | Source |

laggard in use, only in governance.

### APAC — attitudes measured, institutional rates not

The Ipsos *Education Monitor 2026* measures public support for **banning** AI in schools, which
is a political-risk indicator rather than an adoption rate:

| Market | Support for banning AI in schools | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Australia | **52%**, from 47% | Rising |
| Japan | **29%**, from 21% | Rising sharply (+8pp) |
| South Korea | **31%**, from 27% | Rising |
| Indonesia | Fell | Falling |

Support for a ban is **lower across most Asian markets than in Australia and New Zealand**.
The strategic point: Australia has simultaneously the region's **largest proprietary deployment**
(ChatGPT Edu across four universities, announced 5 Nov 2026) and its **highest and rising public
support for prohibition**. Any Australian engagement needs a public-legitimacy and
consent/transparency workstream, not just a technical one.

#### `p874` adds the first APAC-vs-other-region *comparative* figures, and bounds the gap precisely

The **Digital Education Council *AI in Higher Education Global Survey 2026*** — **45,398 responses
across 35 countries** — reports regional splits that no earlier pass had extracted. These are
**individual** measures, and they are the closest thing to an APAC rate that exists:

| Measure | APAC | Comparator | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faculty **excited** about AI's potential for more effective, accessible learning | **57%** | **26%** US & Canada | 🟢 **The largest regional sentiment gap in the file — APAC faculty are 2.2× as positive as North American** |
| **Intent to use AI in teaching** | **92%** | EMEA **89%**, LATAM **94%** | Near-ceiling everywhere; APAC is not an outlier |

🔴 **Methodology caveat that must travel with these numbers:** DEC collects **opt-in through
participating member institutions**, not an audited random sample. Treat as indicative, never as
representative, and **never average them with the UNESCO IESALC institutional figures** — different
respondents, different objects.

🔴 **The gap itself stands, and is now exactly bounded.** What is still missing is an **institutional**
APAC rate — the share of APAC institutions holding an AI *strategy*, the measure UNESCO IESALC
provides for LATAM (**87%** use / **26%** strategy). DEC measures **people**; UNESCO measures
**institutions**; no instrument measures APAC institutions. `p874` also found one hard
institutional *datapoint* rather than a rate: **Nanyang Technological University (Singapore) gave
every undergraduate full access to Gemini Enterprise, Google AI Studio and Vertex AI from August
2026** — a whole-institution commitment of the kind that, counted across the region, would *be*
the missing rate.

**Also `p874`:** the Ipsos Indonesia movement is quantified — support for banning AI in schools
**fell from 30% to 23%**, the clearest liberalising trend in the Monitor.

### EMEA — 🟢 the three-pass gap CLOSES: a European instrument, measuring European students, exists

> 🟢 **`p874` closes this file's longest-standing measurement gap.** Three passes recorded "no
> standalone European sector survey." One exists, it is five weeks old, and it measures European
> secondary students directly.

**OpenAI, *"Closing the AI Guidance Gap in Education"* (published 10 November 2026)**, built on
research by the youth think tank **ThinkYoung**:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | **5,000 secondary students aged 13–19 across 10 European countries**, plus **600 teachers** |
| Fieldwork | **14 April – 15 May 2026** |
| Students using AI tools for homework or assignments | **79%** |
| Students who said someone had **shown them how** to use the tools effectively | 🔴 **47%** |

🔴 **A number collision this file must not let through.** The **79%** above is *European secondary
students doing homework*. The **79%** in the LATAM table is *LATAM higher-education faculty
engaging with AI*. **Same digits, different populations, different objects, different instruments.**
They are not comparable and must never be cited interchangeably.

**A second European instrument, on teacher practice** — the **European School Education Platform
survey (April 2026)**:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Teachers using AI at least partially for **planning and materials** | **73%** |
| Teachers using AI for **creating tests, tasks or rubrics** | **67%** |

**Why the 32-point guidance gap (79% → 47%) is the commercially important number in this file.**
It is not an adoption gap, it is a **capability-and-governance** gap, and it is measured on the
same population in the same instrument — which is what makes it usable in a bid. It also lines up
exactly with the **binding** EU obligation: the AI Act's **AI-literacy duty applies to schools as
deployers now**, while the education high-risk tier is deferred to **2 December 2027**. The
measured deficit and the live legal duty are the same engagement.

🟢 **And there is now a standard to deliver it against — new in `p874`.** The **European Commission
and the OECD published the AI Literacy (AILit) Framework for primary and secondary education on
18 June 2026**: **four dimensions** (how learners *engage with*, *create with*, *manage* and
*shape* AI) and **19 competences** spanning knowledge, skills and attitudes, with learner
expectations, learning scenarios and classroom examples. It is **non-binding**, was developed with
**CodeAI (formerly Code.org)**, endorses no products, and will inform the **OECD's first PISA
assessment of media and AI literacy in 2029**. The Commission's stated rationale was that **68% of
teenagers already use AI** while schools lacked a shared definition. One source dates it 17 June;
the Commission's own page and the majority say 18 June.

**Why AILit matters more than its non-binding status suggests:** it is the first *common
vocabulary* for what the AI Act's literacy duty actually requires. A deployer asked to show it
discharged that duty now has 19 named competences to map training against instead of inventing a
curriculum — and a Commission Education Package was due later in 2026. **This is the artifact an
EMEA AI-literacy engagement should be built on.** See `compose/patterns.md` **P11**.

#### Previous EMEA data points (`p873`) — frequency, retained

The **70% formal-AI-guidance** figure above comes from UNESCO IESALC's comparator rather than a
European instrument. The Digital Education Council reports EMEA only in aggregate. **No standalone
European sector survey (EUA, Jisc or equivalent) has surfaced in three passes** — see declared gaps.

`p873` adds one measurement of a different kind, worth having precisely because it measures
**frequency** rather than ever-use:

| Measure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Academics using generative AI **every week** | **67%** | QS research, via EMEA-context secondary reporting |
| Students using generative AI **every week** | **62%** | Same |

**Why frequency matters more than the headline adoption rates.** The global instruments say 88% of
students and 77% of faculty *use* AI. Weekly use at 62–67% says this is **routine practice, not
experimentation** — which changes what a governance engagement is for. A policy written for
occasional use is the wrong artifact: the need is operational guardrails inside weekly workflows.
**Caveat, and it is a real one:** QS is a global higher-education instrument, not a European one,
and it surfaced here in EMEA-framed reporting. It **narrows** the EMEA gap; it does not close it.

## Opportunities by region

### North America

**Regulatory and procurement picture.** No single federal AI statute. The binding constraint
is **FERPA**, which applies the moment an AI tool touches identifiable student data. Activity
is state-led and fragmented: a tracker counts **134 AI-in-education bills across 31 states in
2026**, concentrated on data privacy, classroom use and curriculum. Federal action is funding-
shaped rather than prohibitive — the **K-12 AI Literacy and Readiness Act of 2026 (H.R. 8747)**
advanced out of committee, and the Department of Education issued a final rule making AI a
**weighted priority across all discretionary grant programs**. Institutional policy is
hardening in both directions: the **SUNY** systemwide policy requires AI-literacy training in
general education for undergraduates entering **fall 2026**, while **Berkeley Law** has barred
AI from all exams and credited coursework; Florida's Board of Governors is weighing mandatory
AI-use disclosure for faculty and students.

**Regulatory detail added by `p872`.** The state picture is now specific enough to name:

- **Four states enacted 2026 laws requiring *both* state-level guidance *and* mandatory
  district-level AI policy adoption: Maryland, Idaho, Oklahoma and Virginia.** This is the
  template other states will copy, and it creates a *deadline-driven* district market.
- **North Carolina Session Law 2026-41** sets a state model-policy deadline of
  **31 December 2026** and local district policies by **30 June 2027**. Dated, binding, and
  roughly six weeks out at the time of this pass.
- **California AB 1159** would bar the use of student data to train AI models — the most
  consequential pending bill for any vendor whose product improves on student interactions.
- **New York A 9190** would restrict classroom AI use to **ninth grade and above**.
- Federal posture is framework-and-funding, not prohibition: the **US Department of Education's
  Office of Educational Technology published a "Framework for Responsible Use and Development of
  Artificial Intelligence in Education" in March 2026**, and the **K-12 AI Literacy and Readiness
  Act of 2026** advanced out of House committee.
- District-level rules are already operative and restrictive in ways vendors underestimate:
  **New York City** guidance uses a **green / yellow / red tier** model, and **Washington DC's
  OSSE** 2026-27 policy — non-mandatory but a clear signal — **bars AI from grading, discipline,
  IEP decisions and teacher evaluations**.
- **A California ballot measure, the "Parents & Kids Safe AI Act",** has been proposed by OpenAI
  and Common Sense Media and could set standards for AI products used by minors, edtech included.
  Ballot-measure risk is a different planning horizon from legislative risk and should be flagged
  to any client building for California minors.

**The DC list is the one to internalise:** grading, discipline, IEP decisions and teacher
evaluation are exactly the four use cases a vendor pitch deck reaches for first, and they are the
four being ruled out. Build toward teacher *augmentation* and learner-facing support, and keep
consequential decisions human.

**Opportunities.**
- **FERPA-defensible self-hosted tutoring.** The combination "we never send student data to a
  model vendor" is directly sellable here. Stack: Ollama or vLLM + LiteLLM + DeepTutor or
  LibreChat, deployed in the institution's own tenancy.
- **Grant-aligned AI-literacy delivery.** AI is now a weighted priority across *all* ED
  discretionary grants, and SUNY-style literacy mandates create a curriculum-build market.
  `microsoft/ai-agents-for-beginners` and `mlabonne/llm-course` are MIT/Apache-2.0 bases that
  can be white-labelled into institutional curricula.
- **Fifty-state policy-compliance tooling.** 134 bills across 31 states is an
  unmanageable-by-hand compliance surface for any multi-state operator — a product-shaped gap.
- **Canvas-adjacent integration.** Canvas dominates US higher ed and is AGPL-3.0. Build
  permissive LTI 1.3 tools (via `ltijs`) that attach to it rather than forking it.
- **Assessment redesign.** Berkeley Law's exam ban and the Florida disclosure debate signal
  demand for assessment that is robust to AI, not merely detection of it. `edx-ora2` plus
  oral/viva and process-evidence workflows is a concrete offering.

### EMEA

**Regulatory picture — the most consequential in the world this year, and it just moved.**
- **Emotion recognition in education is already banned.** AI Act Article 5(1)(f) has applied
  since **2 February 2025**: inferring emotions of natural persons in education settings is
  prohibited except for medical or safety purposes. This is in force now and rules out a whole
  class of engagement-detection and attention-tracking products.
- **High-risk obligations for education were delayed to 2 December 2027.** The Digital Omnibus
  on AI (**Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**) moved admissions, assessment and exam-proctoring
  obligations from 2 August 2026 by roughly 16 months. Sources differ on fine points of the
  adoption timeline, and this session **could not reach `eur-lex.europa.eu`** (see gap below),
  so the primary text is unconfirmed here.
- **Transparency duties were NOT delayed.** Article 50 applies from **2 August 2026**: a
  student-facing chatbot must disclose that it is an AI. This is live.
- **AI literacy (Article 4)** is reported to have been softened by the Omnibus from ensuring a
  level of literacy to taking measures — secondary sources only.

**Opportunities.**
- **The 2027 window is the offer.** Institutions have ~12 months before high-risk obligations
  bite on admissions, assessment and proctoring. "Build it conformant now, not retrofitted in
  2027" is a time-boxed, concrete engagement with a date attached.
- **Article 50 disclosure retrofit** — immediate, small, high-volume work across every
  institution already running a support chatbot.
- **Sovereign, self-hosted deployment.** The UK signal is unusually sharp: Newcastle,
  Westminster and Southampton have reportedly decided **not to renew** commercial AI contracts
  on expiry, citing concerns about entrusting student data to commercial providers and the
  limits of agentic AI marking (via Times Higher Education, secondary). Non-renewal is a
  procurement opening for a self-hosted alternative, and the strongest EMEA demand signal in
  this pass.
- **OpenOLAT and ILIAS are EMEA-native.** OpenOLAT is Apache-2.0 — the permissive LMS base for
  a European build. ILIAS carries the German-speaking public sector.
- **High-risk conformance tooling** (logging, human oversight, accuracy documentation) built
  once against the AI Act and resold across institutions.
- **Accessibility conformance as a bid gate — new in `p873`.** The **European Accessibility Act
  has applied since 28 June 2025**, and the next **EN 301 549** revision aligning it to
  **WCAG 2.2 AA** is reported as due in the Official Journal in late 2026 (secondary-sourced). In
  this KB's whole agent inventory, **only `CAHLR/OATutor` publishes a conformance statement**. The
  winning architecture is therefore to launch the agent as an **LTI 1.3 tool inside a platform
  that already holds a conformance statement** (Moodle core and mobile are stated to be WCAG 2.2
  AA certified — a vendor claim to verify via VPAT/ACR) rather than shipping a bespoke learner UI.
  And the audit failure is usually the **generated content**, not the platform: whatever an AI
  content generator emits inherits the obligation. See `compose/patterns.md` **P10**.
- **UK product-safety standards are a named, dated gate — new in `p873`.** The **Department for
  Education** issued generative-AI **product safety standards for education**, reported
  **January 2026**, and **Ofqual** has published coursework-integrity guidance. For UK schools
  work this is a concrete conformance checklist to build against rather than a governance debate.
- **Gulf engagements start from a rulebook, not a vacuum.** With **KHDA Dubai** guidance already
  issued to private schools, the Middle East motion is **compliance implementation** against
  published rules — a different and faster sale than the North American policy-consolidation work.

#### 🟢 `p874` — the AI-literacy duty is the engagement that is billable NOW, not in 2027

Every EMEA opportunity in this file has been organised around the **2 December 2027** education
high-risk tier. `p874` adds the opportunity that does not wait for it:

- 🔴 **The AI-literacy obligation already binds.** The AI Act's literacy duty applies to **schools
  and institutions as deployers**, and secondary reporting places the duty on them from **July
  2026**. There is no 2027 grace period on this one.
- 🟢 **The deficit is now measured on the buyer's own population:** **79%** of European secondary
  students use AI for schoolwork and only **47%** were ever shown how (OpenAI/ThinkYoung, 5,000
  students, 10 countries). A **32-point** gap, from one instrument, on one population.
- 🟢 **A delivery standard exists:** the **EC/OECD AILit Framework** (18 June 2026) — four
  dimensions, **19 competences**, classroom scenarios, non-binding, product-neutral, and feeding
  **PISA 2029**. It converts "support AI literacy" from an unauditable aspiration into 19 named
  competences a deployer can map training and evidence against.
- 🔵 **National beachheads named in the same reporting:** OpenAI holds education partnerships in
  **Estonia** (the *AI Leap* programme pairs ChatGPT access with teacher training), **Slovakia**,
  **Greece** (a May 2026 framework requiring teacher oversight and data protection) and **Italy** —
  i.e. a government-first, country-level integration strategy. **The competitive consequence:**
  where a hyperscaler has the ministry relationship, the open space is the **independent literacy,
  governance and conformance layer**, not the model contract.
- **Shape of the engagement:** AILit-mapped staff training + a deployer-side literacy evidence
  pack + the governance console from **P6**, delivered now and designed to extend into the 2027
  high-risk conformance work (**P4**). Written up as **P11** in `compose/patterns.md`.


### APAC

**Regulatory picture — four genuinely different models, which is itself the opportunity.**
- **China:** no general AI law but the most binding specific rules already in force —
  generative AI, deep synthesis and recommendation-algorithm measures, with **AI-content
  labelling mandatory since 1 September 2025**.
- **Japan:** promotion-led. The **AI Promotion Act** took effect **4 June 2025** with no direct
  penalties; a new **AI Basic Plan** was adopted **14 July 2026**.
- **Singapore:** voluntary frameworks — IMDA's **Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI**
  launched **January 2026**; MAS is consulting on AI risk-management guidelines.
- **India:** least settled; principles-based and non-statutory, with **IT Amendment Rules 2026**
  in force since **20 February 2026** targeting AI-generated content on platforms.
- **Vietnam — added by `p872`, and the most important APAC development for education AI.**
  Vietnam has enacted an AI Law with a **sectoral high-risk list issued as Decision

#### 🟢 `p874` — Vietnam's clock is now unambiguous, and it is the nearest hard deadline in education AI

`p873` flagged Vietnam's statute as unresolved across sources. **It resolves, and the reading is
consistent across the official English text and multiple independent summaries:**

| Item | Resolved value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | **Law on Artificial Intelligence No. 134/2025/QH15** — 🟢 the `91/2025/QH15` variant is **wrong** |
| Passed by the National Assembly | **10 December 2025** |
| **Entered into force** | 🟢 **1 March 2026** — *already in force*; the "1 Mar **2027**" in circulation was this date mis-stated by a year |
| Transition for systems **already operating** — education, healthcare, finance | 🔴 **18 months → 1 September 2027** |
| Transition for all other fields | 12 months → 1 March 2027 |
| Replaces | the AI chapter of the Digital Technology Industry Law |
| Carve-out | systems a state agency finds **likely to cause serious damage are excluded from the transition** and must comply immediately |

**So the conflicting dates were never a contradiction — they were two different clocks.** 1 March
is *entry into force* (2026), 1 September 2027 is the *education compliance deadline*. Both
circulated without their labels.

🔴 **Strategic consequence: 1 September 2027 is three months EARLIER than the EU's 2 December 2027
education high-risk date.** Any client running an AI education system in Vietnam today is inside a
transition window that closes first. Paired with **Decision 33/2026/QD-TTg** (46 high-risk systems,
effective 15 August 2026), which lists biometric and automated learner monitoring as high-risk,
Vietnam is the **most concretely specified education-AI jurisdiction in the world right now** — and
the one where a de-escalation audit (**P8**) has a dated deadline to sell against.

could not be corroborated. Do not cite it to a client.
